2.5.1 Title, researchers and institution

קול קורא 34/2026 · משרד האנרגיה והתשתיות · יחידת המדען הראשי · מסלול אקדמיה

JouleFlex: A Model-Based Decision and Control Framework for Maximizing Useful AI Work under Dynamic Power Limits

JouleFlex: מודלים ובקרה חכמה למקסום תפוקת AI תחת מגבלות הספק משתנות
Principal Investigator (מנהל הפרויקט): Alexander Apartsin, Senior Lecturer, Department of Computer Science · Institution: Holon Institute of Technology (HIT)

2.5.2 Abstract / תקציר

English (299/300 words)

In July 2026 Israel's Electricity Authority froze new data-center grid-connection requests for 140 days: the queue had reached about 27,000 MW, roughly three times the country's average demand [2]. The binding limit is connection capacity, not generation. The PI's study [1] found that a GPU, the chip that runs AI, has an energy-optimal operating power, the Joule Point, well below its maximum; running at maximum, today's default and the level the grid is sized for, is the energy-worst choice. A capped GPU draws less power, so more GPUs fit behind the same connection. JouleFlex makes this a measured third option between full-power connection and refusal: connect on condition of flexibility.

Existing approaches limit power draw or switch GPUs off; this project asks instead how much useful AI work a given power and energy budget can deliver, a question that yields different operating strategies.

The PI's ELF dataset, the first dense enough to locate the Joule Point, holds about 5,500 measurements over 20 AI models and four GPU types; no public equivalent exists. Operating at the Joule Point uses 29 to 31 percent less energy per task at about 1.2 times the runtime; a fleet simulation on the measured curves delivers the same work with 18 to 45 percent less energy; and on the measured A100 curve a fixed connection delivers up to about 50 percent more AI work per megawatt at the chip, to be confirmed at the facility meter.

The project develops models and tools for operating and planning AI data centers under constrained and variable power. These include power-control and workload-allocation methods, pricing that rewards efficient use of flexible power, fleet planning for power-variable operation, and connection-scenario analyses showing how much of the connection queue firm-plus-flexible connections could serve by region, under technical, economic and policy constraints.

עברית (275/300 מילים)

ביולי 2026 הקפיאה רשות החשמל לתקופה של 140 יום את קבלת הבקשות החדשות לחיבור חוות שרתים לרשת החשמל, לאחר שתור הבקשות הגיע לכ-27,000 מגה-וואט, פי שלושה בקירוב מהביקוש הממוצע לחשמל בישראל [2]. האילוץ הכובל הוא קיבולת החיבור, לא כושר הייצור. מחקר קודם של החוקר הראשי [1] מצא כי למעבד GPU, השבב שמריץ בינה מלאכותית, קיים הספק עבודה אופטימלי מבחינה אנרגטית, ה-Joule Point, הנמוך בהרבה מההספק המרבי שלו; הפעלה בהספק המרבי, ברירת המחדל כיום והערך שלפיו מתוכננת רשת החשמל, היא דרך ההפעלה הגרועה ביותר מבחינה אנרגטית. מעבד מוגבל-הספק צורך פחות הספק, ולכן ניתן להציב יותר מעבדים מאחורי אותו חיבור לרשת. JouleFlex הופך זאת לאפשרות שלישית ומדודה, בין חיבור בהספק מלא לבין סירוב: חיבור בתנאי של גמישות.

הגישות הקיימות מגבילות את צריכת ההספק או מכבות מעבדים; פרויקט זה שואל במקום זאת כמה עבודת AI שימושית ניתן להפיק מתקציב הספק ואנרגיה נתון, שאלה המובילה לאסטרטגיות הפעלה שונות.

מערך הנתונים ELF, המדידה הראשונה הצפופה דיה לאיתור ה-Joule Point, כולל כ-5,500 מדידות על 20 מודלים של AI וארבעה סוגי GPU, ללא מקבילה ציבורית. עבודה בנקודה זו צורכת 29 עד 31 אחוז פחות אנרגיה למשימה, בזמן ריצה ארוך פי 1.2 בקירוב; סימולציית צי על העקומות המדודות מספקת עבודה זהה ב-18 עד 45 אחוז פחות אנרגיה; ועל עקומת ה-A100 המדודה, חיבור קבוע מספק עד כ-50 אחוז יותר עבודת AI לכל מגה-וואט ברמת השבב, ממצא שייבדק במונה המתקן.

הפרויקט יפתח מודלים וכלים להפעלה ולתכנון של חוות שרתים לבינה מלאכותית בתנאי הספק מוגבל ומשתנה: שיטות לבקרת הספק ולהקצאת עומסי חישוב, מנגנוני תמחור המתגמלים ניצול יעיל של הספק גמיש, תכנון צי GPU לעבודה בהספק משתנה, וניתוחי תרחישי חיבור המראים לרגולטור כמה מתור החיבור ניתן לספק תחת משטר חיבורים גמישים, לפי אזור, בהתחשב באילוצים טכניים, כלכליים ורגולטוריים.

2.5.4 Objectives of the program

2.5.4.1 The objective

The problem. In July 2026 the Electricity Authority stopped accepting new data-center connection requests for 140 days: the connection queue had reached about 27,000 MW, roughly three times Israel's average electricity consumption [2]. The binding limit is connection capacity at specific substations, not generation. The Joule Point. A GPU (the chip that runs AI) does its most work per joule below full power: lower its power cap (a software ceiling on its electricity draw) and output falls more slowly than power, so energy per task reaches a minimum, the Joule Point (JP), before rising again. The PI's study [1] measured the JP across 20 models and 4 GPUs: well below the thermal design power (TDP, the card's full rated power), near-constant per card, and 29–31 percent less energy per task at about 1.2 times the runtime. A capped card draws less power, so more cards fit behind the same connection: on the measured A100 curve, up to about 50 percent more AI work per connected megawatt at the chip. Data centers still run cards at maximum power, the energy-worst point, and Israel's grid is sized for it.

The objective is to give the Ministry (policy) and the Electricity Authority (connection licensing) measured tools to license AI load on condition of flexibility, so that each connected megawatt does more useful AI work. The instrument is JouleFlex: models and tools for operating and planning AI data centers under constrained and variable power. Within 24 months the project extends the measured foundation to current hardware, builds the control engine and a simulator that replays the measurements, measures the flexibility a facility can offer, and analyzes the pricing and connection rules, with a stated minimum-success path (2.5.7.1); multi-site deployment follows the grant. Four capabilities, each built on the JP: (C1) Power-control and workload-allocation methods: the power-response law validated on current hardware and whole servers; job and transfer models that place the energy-optimal cap for any workload or unmeasured GPU; and a control engine that assigns jobs and caps together to hold a facility inside its power envelope while meeting deadlines. (C2) Pricing rules evaluated in the simulator that reward efficient use of flexible power instead of per-hour billing, which rewards the energy-worst point. (C3) Fleet-planning methods for power-variable operation: which cards, how many, with what power headroom, deliver the most work behind a variable connection. (C4) Connection-scenario analysis for the regulator: by transmission region, how much of the connection queue could be served under firm-plus-flexible connections at each flexibility level, under technical, economic and policy constraints. A simulator replaying the measured curves evaluates all four; C3 and C4 are scenario analyses run in the simulator on public Noga and Electricity Authority data (milestones in 2.5.7.1); they are studies, not deployed systems; everything is released openly with ELF-2, the extended dataset.

2.5.4.2 The research question

Can an AI data center run as a controllable electrical load under Israeli grid constraints: each GPU near its energy-optimal cap, the facility inside a power limit that changes over time, more AI work per unit of energy and of connected power, and every admitted job meeting its deadline?

Six sub-questions structure the work plan:

  1. Does the law generalize? Do the power law and JP (measured on 20 models, 4 GPUs) hold on H100/L40S-class hardware, production inference software (running a trained AI model to answer requests), and shared GPUs?
  2. When is one cap per card enough, and where must a job model replace the near-constant per-card JP?
  3. Where is the true optimum at the wall? What is the JP at the server's electrical input, which the grid meters, not just the board?
  4. Can we skip the measurement? Can an unmeasured GPU's JP be predicted from published specs plus at most one short test?
  5. Can allocation and power be optimized together? Can job placement and per-card caps be chosen jointly under a time-varying power limit with every deadline met, and which card mix and power headroom deliver the most work behind a variable connection (C1, C3)?
  6. How much flexibility can a facility sell? How much power can be shed, how fast, for how long, at what notice and cost; which pricing rules make flexibility profitable; and how much of the queue, by transmission region, firm-plus-flexible connections could serve (C2, C4)?

2.5.5 The proposed research

2.5.5.1 Scientific and technological background

The Israeli challenge is specific and pressing. Data centers, with AI the main growth driver, approach 945 TWh of electricity demand by 2030 [3]; Israel's grid is isolated, with no cross-border connections, so the 27,000 MW queue [2] makes connected megawatts, not energy, the binding national limit; today's operating decisions think in watts, not joules.

The discovery. The PI's study [1] (the ELF dataset) mapped how energy per task depends on the power cap: board power follows a simple empirical law in the computing rate θ,

P(θ) = P0 + a·θβ,   median R2 = 0.99,

a fixed floor P₀ plus a superlinear term, so energy per task, E = P/θ, is U-shaped in the cap with its minimum, the JP, at 43 to 46 percent of rated power on the large GPUs; the cap is one software command, fast enough to follow grid signals [1]. These are GPU board numbers; server-input (BMC) power adds host overhead but excludes cooling and building losses (PUE), so WP1 and WP4 confirm the facility megawatt the grid sees.

2.5.5.2 Review of existing knowledge

Four research lines meet here. Energy-proportional computing [4] named the fixed, work-independent part of server power the central inefficiency; our floor P₀ measures it for GPUs. Speed-scaling theory proved that energy is minimized at a sub-maximal speed [5,6,7]; ELF gives the measured form for GPU inference. GPU energy systems, Zeus [8] and the ML.ENERGY benchmarks [9,10], search for a setting per job at runtime; the per-card-constant JP replaces the search with one measurement. Production context: cluster studies [11] and public traces [12] supply job arrivals; energy-aware GPU schedulers [13,14] leave device power at its default; methodology reviews [15] motivate the fixed protocol. Recent work power-caps inference servers at one recommended cap [20], finds capping ineffective for memory-bound decode [23], and runs AI data centers as grid assets by following a facility power target [21]; 2.5.5.6 states where JouleFlex departs from each.

2.5.5.3 Knowledge gaps

  • The law's reach and job dependence are unmapped. The law is established for single jobs at fixed settings on three finely swept GPUs (A100, A10G, L4); its persistence on H100 and B200-class hardware, production serving software and shared cards is unknown; the per-card constant holds only when the GPU is busy enough that the cap binds, no model predicts a job's operating point from its features, and placing the JP on a new GPU still requires a full sweep [1].
  • All published numbers are board numbers. The grid meters the facility; host overhead and power-supply losses shift the optimum upward by an amount WP1 measures; the chip-level megawatt figure awaits facility confirmation.
  • Allocation and power control are not jointly optimized. The 18 to 45 percent fleet saving used a fixed budget, a fixed placement rule and caps chosen afterward; the joint problem, coupling discrete scheduling to continuous control through one power budget, is unsolved.
  • Flexibility is unquantified. How much power an AI facility can shed, how fast, for how long, at what cost has never been measured or standardized; the Authority's hearing [19] is open and these numbers are not yet in it.
  • The incentive problem is identified but unsolved. Per-hour billing makes the renter's cheapest choice the energy-worst point, and a per-joule surcharge is too weak [1]; which structural instruments work is open.

2.5.5.4 Description of the proposed research

JouleFlex builds one measured foundation and four tools on it: WP1–WP2 measure and predict the energy-optimal operating point per job and card; WP3 controls allocation and power in real time in a digital twin; WP4–WP5 connect to the grid, defining the flexibility a facility can offer and the rules that reward it.

Output: the policy report and the open release of the dataset, control engine, twin, and interfaces.

2.5.5.6 Novelty and originality

  • AI load as a grid resource, grounded in the measured optimum. Unlike grid-following curtailment to a power target [21], JouleFlex runs each card at its measured energy-optimal cap and makes the resulting flexibility contractable and audited, quantified in MW, ramp, hold duration and recovery.
  • Energy first, power as the means. Inference power-capping saves energy at a chosen cap [20] and can be ineffective for memory-bound decode [23]; JouleFlex sets power to the computed energy optimum per (card, job) where the cap binds, and every recommendation carries its capital cost in extra cards.
  • An open dataset with no equivalent. WattGPU [22] and ML.ENERGY [9,10] report inference power at one operating point and open power-cap sweeps cover training or HPC; no sweep dense enough to locate the inference energy optimum existed before ELF.
  • The joint allocation-and-capping problem, to the measured optimum. Placing jobs and setting every cap at the energy optimum is a joint problem grid-following controllers [21] do not address; the JouleFlex engine is a first solution on measured curves.

2.5.5.8 Preliminary results

The preliminary results are the applicant's study The Joule Point [1] and its ELF dataset: about 5,500 instrumented measurements over 20 models and four GPUs (A100, A10G, L4 swept finely, T4 coarsely). Headline numbers: 29 to 31 percent less energy per task at the JP (43 to 46 percent of TDP, runs 1.2 times longer); 18 to 45 percent less energy per served job in a measured-curve fleet simulation at equal-or-better deadline compliance; cap actuation settling in about 0.2 s (191 ms measured). Supporting: law fit median R² = 0.99 per fixed-setting sweep (pooled per-card fits 0.88 to 0.92, Fig. A1); one static cap per card serves all twenty workloads at under one percent mean penalty; under strict deadlines capping saved 28 percent of energy while keeping 95 percent of workload value; under per-instance-hour billing the renter's cost optimum is the energy-worst operating point. The measurement software, the P(θ) fitting pipeline, the exact per-card capping solver and the fleet simulator behind these numbers are the PI's working code, and ELF is openly released, so every figure here is independently reproducible; WP1–WP3 extend this foundation.

2.5.6 Application: expected benefit and impact

2.5.6.1 What the project delivers, concretely

The project delivers five open artifacts: the ELF-2 dataset, the operating-point models with their one-test protocol, the control engine, the simulator built from the measurements, and the grid interfaces and policy report (Appendix Table A1: what each is, who uses it). They carry C1 (models, engine, simulator), C2 (policy report), C3 (simulator-based fleet planning) and C4 (queue scenario analysis by transmission region); all are released openly on delivery, crediting the Ministry's R&D program under call 34/2026 [18].

2.5.6.2 Benefit and impact

For the Israeli grid and regulator (the primary benefit). The freeze [2] affects four parties. The Ministry owns the policy and the freeze decision, with no measured basis for how much AI load can flex. The Electricity Authority (רשות החשמל), which licenses connections, must either grant connections the grid cannot back at specific substations, or keep the queue closed and block growth. Noga, the system operator, must hold the grid stable against a large, new and unmeasured load. The operators in the queue cannot build. None has the numbers to license AI load on condition of flexibility: how much it can shed, how fast, for how long, at what notice and cost. JouleFlex produces exactly those numbers.

The third option rests on one fact: more work per joule is more work per connected megawatt, if the operator fills the same connection with more cards. A fixed-connection calculation on the measured A100 curve implies up to about 50 percent more AI work per megawatt at the GPU [1], to be confirmed at server input and facility level before any coefficient is recommended; the 27,000 MW queue includes speculative and duplicated applications, so any coefficient applies to approved capacity. WP5 puts a shekel-per-megawatt value on the flexible-connection option from public data, feeding the Authority's open hearing [19] while the rules are written. Connection constraints are locational, per substation and transmission region, so C4 is a scenario analysis by region of how much of the queue could be served under firm-plus-flexible connections at each flexibility level; with flexibility measured, committed and audited (WP4), the closed queue stops being all-or-nothing.

What the Authority could write into the 50 MVA connection track [19]. Connection coefficient. A facility committing to Joule-Point operation is licensed at k times the nameplate of its installed hardware, where k is its measured power at the Joule Point as a fraction of nameplate; WP1 measures k at server input and carries it to the facility meter with cooling and building losses (PUE), and the WP4 delivery record verifies it in operation. Worked example: a 100 MW applicant that commits to JP operation keeps a 100 MW connection and installs 100/k MW of nameplate; at the board value k = 0.45 [1] that is about 220 MW of hardware, delivering on the measured A100 curve up to about 50 percent more AI work than 100 MW uncapped. The facility k is higher, since host and cooling power do not fall with the cap. Curtailment product in the system operator's direction. A facility drops from its uncapped operating draw to the Joule Point on Noga's instruction, giving up roughly 40 percent of its operating power for about 20 percent of its speed, specified by MW shed against the measured uncapped draw (the MS0 memo fixes the exact per-card fraction), ramp (seconds), hold duration (hours, through the evening peak), notice time, maximum events per season, recovery, work lost and cost; the JP-and-below shed is a smaller second tier, each quantity measured by WP4 at the connection point and audited by the delivery record.

Training and human capital. The project builds research capacity at the AI-energy interface, which Israel lacks: a postdoctoral researcher trained across GPU energy measurement, ML-systems control and energy-market analysis; the open ELF-2 dataset, simulator and control engine as teaching material at HIT; and the Hebrew M4, M8 and M21 briefings to Ministry, Authority, Noga and operators.

For operators and planners. Operators (C1): one software command delivers 29 to 31 percent lower energy per task (measured) and 18 to 45 percent lower energy per served job (fleet simulation) on installed hardware; C2 pricing in place of per-hour billing lets a customer who accepts JP operation or curtailment windows pay less per unit of work. Planners (C3): the simulator shows, before capital is committed, which fleet delivers the most work behind a variable connection.

6. תקורה

תקורה בשיעור 15% מהתמורה עבור הוצאות המחקר, למעט סעיף קבלני משנה, בהתאם ל-§7.1. התקורה מכסה הוצאות עקיפות ובכללן שירותי מזכירות, ראיית חשבון, משאבי אנוש, תחזוקת חשבונות מחקר, ניהול המחקר, שימוש בספריות, גישה לשירותי מחשב, שימוש במתקני מחקר ובמשרדים, ומים, אנרגיה ולוגיסטיקה. הוצאות אלה אינן נרשמות בנפרד במפרט (§7.3).

שיעור התקורה במכון הטכנולוגי חולון עומד על 15%, הזהה לתקרה שקובע הקול הקורא, ולכן התקציב לעיל משקף את התקורה המוסדית בפועל ואין צורך בהתאמה.
